# What good looks like

Distilled from recruiters and from designers who landed roles this way (next play newsletter, "23 recruiters from top startups", Oct 2026; Dive Talent post on Jason Marsh's "spearfishing" playbook). Paraphrased.

## The idea

Stop telling, start showing. Pretend you already work there, do a weekend of real work, and send it to the person who would be your manager. This puts you in a different pile because it shows what working with you looks like, not what you claim.

## Picking the problem

- It must be a problem they actually have now, backed by something you can point at: a broken flow, a support thread, a review, a competitor doing it better, a gap between what they launched and what users ask for.
- Solve a piece, not the whole. A slice of one flow beats a redesign of the product.
- It should look like the job. Read the role description and pick a problem in the territory that role owns. A prototype that matches what the team is working on right now calibrates with them; one outside their lane reads as a hobby.
- Ask what their actual gaps are, not what generic best practice says. Generic advice is the thing recruiters said they are tired of.
- Jason Marsh's discovery method: screenshot the flow, rebuild it faithfully, then stare at the rebuild. Reconstruction forces you to notice what you skim past when using the product.
- Never pitch what exists. A prototype of a feature the company already ships tells the hiring manager you did not use the product. The offerings inventory in stage 1 and the overlap check in stage 4 exist for this; when something slips through anyway, it is named in the digest, not buried.
- Problems are one source of concepts, not the only one. The applicant's instruction is to also come up with ideas that make the product better and with new offerings the company could add, grounded in where the company says it is going. A fix shows craft; an improvement shows judgment; a new offering shows you understand the business.

## Sizing the prototype

- A weekend of work. If the scope needs more, cut it.
- Working, not static. Real interactions, real content shaped like theirs, no lorem ipsum.
- It must feel like it came from inside their team: their tokens, their type, their copy voice, their component shapes.
- One sweated detail that an AI could not have one-shotted. In 2026 a generic AI prototype carries no signal; the detail is the signal. The agent's job is to get the slice working and on-brand, then name the details worth the human's hand. The human finishes.
- Lighter formats exist for a first touch: a platform teardown, a positioning teardown, five user interviews synthesized. The prototype is the designer's default.

## Delivering it

Out of scope for this plugin beyond the note, but it shapes the note:

- It goes straight to the hiring manager or founder, never through the application portal.
- One honest sentence linking your why to theirs.
- Be direct about how your past work translates and where your gaps are. Recruiters said this builds trust rather than costing it.
- Frame it as "here is my take, I would love your read", not "here is what you got wrong". You are asking for feedback and calibration, not delivering a verdict.

## What fails

- Claims without evidence. Generic best practice. Spraying applications.
- Anything an AI could produce without having used the product.
- A full unsolicited redesign. Reads as presumptuous and ignores constraints you cannot see.
- Research that misreads the business. Everything downstream inherits the error, so stage 1 gets the most scrutiny.

## Caveats about these sources

Both sources are hiring-side success stories. Nothing in them covers what got ignored, so treat the tone guidance above (small, curious, asks for a read) as a guardrail, not optional.
